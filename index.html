The risk

Keys, not location, decide the answer

A UK data centre region tells you where a server stands. It does not tell you which companies can be ordered to act.

Control of the key, not the location of the server, decides what a demand on a provider can produce.

Almost every regulated firm now encrypts its sensitive data. The harder question is who holds the key. If the cloud company that stores your data also generates, stores and can use the key, a legal demand served on that company can reach both. If you hold the key yourself, somewhere the cloud company cannot reach, the same demand produces only scrambled data.

In 2018 the CLOUD Act added section 2713 to Title 18 of the United States Code. It requires a provider to disclose information within its "possession, custody, or control, regardless of whether such communication, record, or other information is located within or outside of the United States" (18 U.S.C. § 2713). The duty follows what the provider controls, and it does not stop at the US border. A US-owned cloud provider running a London region remains a US company. If that provider holds your keys, a US demand on the provider can reach them.

There are safeguards. Under section 2703(h) a provider may ask a US court to change or cancel a demand, and must file within 14 days of being served. The UK–US Data Access Agreement, in force since 3 October 2022, limits orders to serious crime, and orders "may not intentionally target" a person located in the other country. I record both in every report as factors that reduce exposure. Neither moves the key out of the provider's hands.

This is general information about what the texts say, not legal advice. How a particular demand would be treated depends on facts and on law that courts continue to develop, and that is a matter for a firm's own counsel.

The method

Evidence, not claims

The assessment scores what can be proved, and one gate stops good paperwork hiding a provider that can decrypt alone. The two tiers differ in scope, not in method.

From answers to a rating

Each area's score is the average of its question scores, expressed as a percentage of the maximum. The overall Key Sovereignty Score, from 0 to 100, is the weighted sum of the eight area scores. A second consultant checks every score.

The structural gate

If the provider can decrypt any sensitive in-scope data without a call to a service you control, the overall rating cannot be better than Elevated, however well the other areas score. A polished policy pack cannot conceal a structural gap.

Example: a mutual insurer averages 60.8, inside the Moderate band. Its policy and claims database keys sit in the provider's own key service, so the gate caps the rating at Elevated. Once the master key moves into hardware the insurer controls, and the evidence shows it, the gate lifts.

Why this matters now

A fixed date and a written answer

The PRA has set a date

The PRA expects banks and insurers to weigh risks to data access arising from "local law enforcement, legal, or political circumstances", and lists "encryption and key management" among the data controls it expects. The updated supervisory statement takes effect on 18 March 2027.

PRA SS2/21, updated March 2026

DORA asks where data sits

For firms with EU operations, DORA has applied since 17 January 2025. ICT contracts must state "the locations, namely the regions or countries, where the contracted or subcontracted functions and ICT services are to be provided and where data is to be processed, including the storage location".

Regulation (EU) 2022/2554, Articles 30 and 64

A UK region is not the answer

A legal duty to produce data attaches to the company that holds or controls it, not to the building. A risk owner asked "where are our keys, and who could be compelled to produce them?" needs an answer that stands up to a regulator's follow-up questions.

18 U.S.C. § 2713
